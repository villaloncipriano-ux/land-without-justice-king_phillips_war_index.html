Land Without Justice

Law, Violence, and the Making of King Philip’s War, 1620–1676

King Philip’s War was not a sudden eruption of violence. It was the predictable end of a colonial system in which “legality” — treaties, deeds, and courts — functioned as the polite face of an expansion that depended on force. When Indigenous nations pushed back, colonists abandoned legality almost overnight.

A presentation in eleven sections, with a timeline and a closing bibliography.
Introduction

The Problem

In June 1675, three Wampanoag men were tried and hanged in Plymouth Colony for the murder of John Sassamon — a Christian Indian who had warned colonists that Metacom was preparing for war. Within weeks, the conflict the colonists would name after Metacom, whom they called “King Philip,” had engulfed New England.

By the time the fighting ended fourteen months later, dozens of English towns lay in ruins, thousands of Indigenous people were dead or sold into Caribbean slavery, and Metacom’s severed head was on a pike at Plymouth, where it would remain for two decades. Per capita, it remains one of the costliest wars in what would become the United States.

This presentation traces how a half-century of colonial “legal” expansion — treaties, land deeds, court hearings — collapsed almost overnight into total war, and argues that the legal architecture and the violence that replaced it were never really opposites. They were the same project at different temperatures.

Background · Before the War

Plymouth, Massasoit, and the Treaty of 1621

When the Plymouth colonists arrived in 1620, they did not encounter unspoiled wilderness. They encountered communities already devastated by an epidemic that had swept the New England coast between 1616 and 1619, killing a staggering portion of the Wampanoag and Pawtuxet populations. Massasoit (Ousamequin), sachem of the Wampanoag, faced this crisis from a weakened position.

In March 1621, he agreed to a mutual-aid treaty with the Plymouth settlers — a brief document of six articles promising non-aggression and shared defense. For Massasoit, the agreement was a strategic alliance against the Narragansett to the west. For the colonists, it would later be cited — retroactively — as a foundation for claims of English sovereignty over Wampanoag land.

As Lisa Brooks argues in Our Beloved Kin, the diplomatic relationships forged by Massasoit and his sons reflected longstanding Wampanoag traditions of alliance — networks of kinship, not subordination. But colonial recordkeepers translated those relationships into a vocabulary of subjects and lords. Massasoit kept the peace for forty years. When he died around 1661, his eldest son Wamsutta succeeded him; Wamsutta died suddenly soon after being summoned to Plymouth, and his younger brother Metacom — Philip — took up the leadership of the Pokanoket Wampanoag in 1662.

“That neither he nor any of his should injure or do hurt to any of their people.” — Article I of the 1621 Plymouth–Wampanoag Treaty (paraphrased into modern English from the surviving record)
The Illusion of “Legal” Expansion

How “Sales” Were Manufactured

Throughout the 1640s, ’50s, and ’60s, English Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay absorbed enormous tracts of Wampanoag, Massachusett, and Nipmuc territory through what the colonial records describe as voluntary “deeds” and “purchases.” In Changes in the Land, William Cronon argues that these transactions were built on a fundamental category mistake: English law treated land as a permanent, alienable commodity, while Indigenous nations understood agreements as conferring use rights — fishing, planting, hunting — rather than absolute, exclusive ownership.

A “sale” recorded in a Plymouth ledger was, on the Wampanoag side, often closer to a season’s lease. The two parties signed the same paper but did not sign the same agreement.

Even within English terms, the transactions were coercive. Sachems who refused to sign were fined in colonial courts for unrelated offenses. Newly imported English livestock — cattle, hogs, horses — managed by colonists who fenced poorly and let them roam, destroyed Indigenous cornfields; colonists then bought the damaged land cheaply. Schultz and Tougias note in King Philip’s War that the legal forms were observed scrupulously even as the substance was extraction.

Metacom, summoned again and again to Plymouth to explain himself, watched his territory shrink with each “agreement.” By 1671 he had been forced to sign a humiliating accord at Taunton: surrender of arms, fines, formal subjection to Plymouth.

“Their cattle and horses still increased; when they removed thirty miles from where they used to live, the English would follow them.” — Metacom’s grievances, recorded by Quaker mediator John Easton, 1675
Rising Tensions

Three Pressures Stack Up

By the early 1670s, three pressures had piled onto the Wampanoag and their neighbors at the same time. Any one would have been destabilizing. Together, they were unsustainable.

1 · Land

Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay had fenced, plowed, and settled most of the arable coastal land. Indigenous communities were being squeezed onto smaller, lower-quality territory, and the colonial demand for new land was accelerating, not slowing.

2 · Sovereignty

Colonial courts increasingly tried Indigenous people under English law — including for offenses defined in religious terms (Sabbath-breaking, “blasphemy”). Indigenous defendants stood before juries that almost never convicted English defendants for the same kinds of harms in reverse.

3 · Culture

The “praying town” system, run by missionaries like John Eliot, pressured Indigenous communities to convert to Christianity, abandon kinship structures, and resettle in colonial-supervised villages. For Wampanoag and Nipmuc leaders, conversion was indistinguishable from political subjection.

In January 1675, John Sassamon — a Christian Wampanoag, Harvard-educated, who had reportedly warned Plymouth that Metacom was assembling a coalition — was found dead beneath the ice at Assawompset Pond. Plymouth tried three Wampanoag men for his murder, convicted them on contested testimony, and hanged them in June. The execution was the spark.

Outbreak of Violence

Negotiations Collapse in Days

Within days of the Sassamon hangings, Wampanoag warriors raided the colonial town of Swansea on June 24, 1675. Plymouth militia mobilized; within two months the war had pulled in Nipmuc, Pocumtuck, and eventually Narragansett combatants, and had spread across what is now Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Connecticut.

The most striking thing about the early summer of 1675 is how fast the diplomatic options closed. The Quaker mediator John Easton, deputy governor of Rhode Island, met with Metacom in mid-June and recorded his grievances in A Relation of the Indian War: that English livestock destroyed Indigenous crops, that drunken sachems were tricked into signing deeds, that English juries convicted Indigenous defendants of capital crimes against colonists but never the reverse. Easton tried to propose arbitration. Plymouth refused.

Lisa Brooks, in Our Beloved Kin, emphasizes that Indigenous leaders did not want a war of annihilation. They wanted the legal system to actually function as a legal system — impartial, reciprocal, accountable. When the colonists made it clear that arbitration was not on offer, the alternative arrived very quickly.

Shift to Total Violence

How Quickly the Legal Frame Was Abandoned

The war became, very quickly, a contest of mutual destruction. By December 1675, English forces had carried out the Great Swamp Massacre against the Narragansett — a nation that had not, up to that point, joined the war — burning a fortified village in present-day Rhode Island and killing between three hundred and a thousand noncombatants. In response, allied Indigenous forces destroyed colonial settlements at Lancaster, Medfield, Marlborough, Sudbury, Providence, and a dozen others. By spring 1676 the frontier line of English settlement had retreated more than fifty miles.

Schultz and Tougias note that, per capita, the war is among the costliest in American history for the colonial side. But the Indigenous toll was catastrophic in a different register entirely. Colonial forces, together with the Mohegan and Pequot allies they had retained, captured and sold an estimated one thousand Wampanoag, Narragansett, and Nipmuc people into Caribbean slavery. Metacom himself was killed at Mount Hope on August 12, 1676; his head was carried back to Plymouth and displayed on a pike for nearly twenty years.

The legal forms — trials, deeds, treaties — that had structured fifty years of colonial expansion were abandoned in months. Captives were not tried; they were sold. Treaties were not negotiated; they were imposed. Land was not deeded; it was forfeited. The “praying towns” that had supposedly offered legal protection to converted Indigenous Christians were dissolved, and their inhabitants were interned on Deer Island in Boston Harbor through the winter of 1675–76, where many died of exposure and starvation.

Analysis · The Central Argument

Law and Violence Were Never Opposites

The legal architecture of colonial New England and the violence of King Philip’s War were not opposites. They were the same project at different temperatures.

In the decades before the war, the legal forms — court hearings, signed deeds, treaty meetings — gave colonists a moral and political vocabulary for expansion. Lands taken under coercion could be entered into the Plymouth records as voluntary sales. Indigenous defendants tried under English law could be hanged with the procedural assurance of due process. Alan Taylor, in American Colonies, characterizes this dynamic as the consistent pattern of English settlement: legal procedure as the public ledger, force as the silent guarantor.

When Indigenous resistance grew strong enough to threaten the ledger, the guarantor came forward. Trials gave way to summary execution. Deeds gave way to confiscation. Diplomacy gave way to internment and enslavement.

Lisa Brooks, in Our Beloved Kin, frames the shift more pointedly: from the Indigenous side, there was no “shift” at all. The violence had been there throughout, exercised under other names — debt, fines, livestock damage, conversion pressure — and the war was simply the moment that violence finally took an English face.

The legal system did not fail in 1675. It functioned exactly as designed: it gave the conquest its paperwork, and stepped aside the moment the paperwork ran out. — Synthesis of Cronon, Brooks, Taylor, and Schultz & Tougias
Consequences · (There Always Are)

Who Inherited New England

Of the roughly eleven thousand Indigenous people in southern New England before the war, recent estimates suggest fewer than four thousand remained free in the region by 1680. Wampanoag, Narragansett, and Nipmuc survivors were dispersed to the north, absorbed into Mohegan and Pennacook communities, or sold abroad. Surviving “praying” Indigenous Christians lost most of their lands; Deer Island internment alone killed hundreds.

For the colonists, the consequences were paradoxical. Massachusetts Bay’s aggressive prosecution of the war alarmed the Crown, and Charles II revoked the colony’s charter in 1684, replacing self-government with the short-lived royal Dominion of New England. But colonial expansion accelerated. By 1700, English settlement had reclaimed and exceeded its pre-war footprint — this time with no Indigenous polity in southern New England capable of checking it again.

Schultz and Tougias close their account by emphasizing this paradox: the war that broke the colonists’ legal pretensions also removed the only remaining barrier to their physical expansion. The post-1676 New England landscape was, for the first time, a fully colonial one.

Broader Pattern

This Was Not a One-Time Thing

The pattern that played out in 1675 — legal expansion as the velvet glove, total war as the iron fist — would repeat. In 1763, a coalition led by the Ottawa leader Pontiac rose against British forts and settlements in the Great Lakes region, in direct response to British policies that abrogated French-era diplomatic protections. The British answer was a familiar mixture: imposed treaties, forced removals, and, at Fort Pitt, the documented attempt to spread smallpox through gifted blankets.

In the early nineteenth century, the United States used the same combination — purchase, treaty, removal, war — across the Old Northwest, the Southeast (the Trail of Tears), and the Plains. Alan Taylor’s argument in American Colonies generalizes the case: the legal-violent hybrid that defined New England in 1675 became the operating model of North American settler expansion for the next two centuries. Different actors, different decades, but a recognizable script.

King Philip’s War is sometimes taught as a tragic local episode. Read against Pontiac’s War, the Creek Wars, the Seminole Wars, the Black Hawk War, the Sioux Wars — it looks less like an episode than like a template.

Conclusion

Why This Matters

King Philip’s War is sometimes taught as a tragic local episode — a failure of communication, a breakdown of an otherwise functioning diplomatic relationship. The evidence suggests something less consolatory.

The war was the predictable conclusion of a system that used legal procedure to legitimize what could not have been legitimized otherwise, and that turned to overt violence the moment legality stopped delivering the result the colonists wanted. The treaties and deeds and court records did not fail in 1675. They were the receipts of a process that had been violent the whole time. The war was simply the receipts catching up to the reality.

Understanding King Philip’s War clearly is, in part, learning to recognize that pattern when it appears under other names later in American history — in Pontiac’s War, in the Trail of Tears, in the broken treaties of the nineteenth century. The names change. The structure does not.

The legal architecture and the violence that replaced it were never really opposites. They were the same project, at different temperatures. — Thesis of this presentation
Bibliography

Sources & Themes

Each entry below indicates the central themes the source contributes to this presentation.

King Philip’s War: The History and Legacy of America’s Forgotten Conflict

Schultz, Eric B., and Michael J. Tougias. King Philip’s War: The History and Legacy of America’s Forgotten Conflict. Countryman Press, 1999.

Themes · Narrative of the war · Fragility of colonial agreements · Diplomacy to violence

This book provides a detailed narrative of King Philip’s War, its causes, key figures, and devastating consequences for both colonists and Indigenous tribes. It is useful for understanding how tensions over land and colonial expansion escalated into widespread violence. The authors highlight the fragility of colonial “agreements” and show how quickly diplomacy flips to violence, supporting the argument that so-called legal systems could not sustain colonial ambitions without force.

Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England

Cronon, William. Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England. Hill and Wang, 1983.

Themes · Land use · Property regimes · Misunderstood treaties

Cronon examines how English colonists and Indigenous peoples held fundamentally different understandings of land use and ownership. This source helps explain why treaties and land “sales” were so often unequal or misunderstood. It supports the claim that colonial legal frameworks masked deeper conflicts over land and sovereignty, ultimately contributing to the breakdown that led to war.

Our Beloved Kin: A New History of King Philip’s War

Brooks, Lisa. Our Beloved Kin: A New History of King Philip’s War. Yale University Press, 2018.

Themes · Indigenous perspective · Sovereignty · Resistance as response

Brooks offers a perspective centered on Indigenous experiences, particularly those connected to Metacom and other Native leaders. This source is valuable for showing how Indigenous resistance was rooted in survival and sovereignty, rather than a sudden rebellion. It strengthens the project by demonstrating that violence was not inevitable from the Indigenous side, but was often a response to colonial pressure and dispossession.

American Colonies: The Settling of North America

Taylor, Alan. American Colonies: The Settling of North America. Penguin Books, 2001.

Themes · Continental context · Patterns of expansion · Recurring conflict

Taylor provides broad context on colonial expansion across North America, including patterns of land acquisition and conflict. This book helps situate King Philip’s War within a larger trend, showing that the combination of “legal” expansion and violence was not isolated. It supports the concluding idea that similar dynamics appeared repeatedly in later American Indian conflicts.
